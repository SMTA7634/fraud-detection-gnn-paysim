Section 2 — Edge construction rules
🔗

Rule 1 — Shared recipient

Two transactions connect if they share the same nameDest account. This captures the same money destination — often a mule account receiving multiple fraudulent transfers.

⏱

Rule 2 — Time window ≤ 30 steps

Only transactions within 30 simulated hours of each other connect. Prevents a recipient with 743 hours of activity from creating millions of irrelevant edges.

↔

Rule 3 — Undirected

Edges go both ways. A fraud transaction at step 100 is relevant context for a legit transaction at step 112 going to the same account — and vice versa.

👤

Note — Senders had zero edges (50k)

In the 50k subset, every nameOrig appeared exactly once, so no sender-based edges formed. Only recipient-based edges exist (496 total).

Section 3a — The 12 node features (standardised)

What each transaction node knows

# Feature Example Notes
1 step 100 raw Simulated hour, 1–743
2 amount 9.19 raw log1p($9,800) — skew reduction
3 oldbalanceOrg 9.19 raw Sender balance, log1p
4 oldbalanceDest 0.00 raw Recipient balance, log1p. 0 = new account
5 hour_of_day 4 eng step % 24
6 day_of_sim 4 eng step ÷ 24
7 pct_balance_used 1.00 eng amount ÷ oldbalanceOrg. 1.0 = account drained
8 type_CASH_IN 0 OHE
9 type_CASH_OUT 0 OHE
10 type_DEBIT 0 OHE
11 type_PAYMENT 0 OHE
12 type_TRANSFER 1 OHE Only TRANSFER & CASH_OUT have fraud

StandardScaler applied after split — mean and std calculated on training nodes only, then used to scale all 50k nodes. Prevents data leakage from test/OOT statistics.

Section 3b — GraphSAGE message passing

How T1 (fraud) aggregates its neighbours

After Layer 1, T1's representation now includes a summary of T2 and T3's features.

After Layer 2, it also includes summaries of T2 and T3's own neighbours — a 2-hop view of the local graph.

A fraud node surrounded by other fraud nodes will produce a very different representation than an isolated fraud node — the GNN learns to use this difference.

Section 4 — Why graph density matters
Why 250k is the right next step for Model 3: The GNN's structural advantage only materialises when fraud transactions share neighbours. In the 50k subset with 496 edges, 98% of nodes are isolated — message passing contributes almost nothing, and the GNN effectively reduces to a plain feed-forward network. Scaling to 250k will create a meaningfully denser graph where the GNN can start to detect fraud clusters.
Current Model 3 limitation explained: OOT F1 = 0.9423 vs XGBoost's 0.9997. The GNN is architecturally correct and ready — it just needs a denser graph to show its advantage. The 50k result is not a GNN failure; it's a data-sparsity constraint.
